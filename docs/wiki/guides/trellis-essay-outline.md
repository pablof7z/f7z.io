# Trellis Essay Outline

## Follow-Up Essay: Divergent Angle Framings

The follow-up Trellis essay can be approached from any of eight divergent angles. Each framing below includes a full title, summary, TL;DR, rationale, why-pick-this-one, and a 5–8 beat outline.

### 1. The Oracle
**Title:** The Oracle
**Summary:** Trellis positions the writer as an oracle—not predicting the future, but surfacing what they already know but haven't yet articulated.
**TL;DR:** The essay you can't write is already inside you. Trellis is the oracle that drags it out.
**Rationale:** The gap between thought and expression is not a knowledge gap; it's a retrieval gap. Most writers fail not because they lack ideas but because they can't locate the ones they have.
**Why pick this one:** It reframes the writing tool as a mirror rather than a scaffold, which is a sharper and more honest pitch.
**Outline (6 beats):**
1. The feeling of knowing something you can't say.
2. Why traditional outlining fails—it asks you to organize before you've found.
3. The oracle metaphor: not prophecy, retrieval.
4. How Trellis's branching questions act as divination rods.
5. The moment the writer reads their own generated node and recognizes it.
6. The essay that was always there, now visible.

### 2. The Price Tag
**Title:** The Price Tag
**Summary:** Every unwritten essay has a cost—the thing you didn't build, the argument you didn't make, the influence you didn't exert. Trellis lowers the price.
**TL;DR:** Not writing isn't free. It's the most expensive thing you do.
**Rationale:** People resist writing tools because writing feels like work, but not writing is silently more expensive. Reframing inaction as a cost changes the calculus.
**Why pick this one:** It weaponizes loss aversion, which is the strongest behavioral lever for getting people to act.
**Outline (7 beats):**
1. The hidden invoice of unwritten ideas.
2. What your unread audience would have done differently.
3. The compounding cost of a thought left in draft.
4. Why traditional writing tools make the price feel higher than it is.
5. Trellis as a cost-cutter: the five-minute path from idea to artifact.
6. The ROI of a published essay vs. a lingering notion.
7. The receipt: what you get back when you finally write it.

### 3. The Shadow
**Title:** The Shadow
**Summary:** Every essay casts a shadow—the arguments it implies but doesn't make, the counterpoints it sidesteps, the deeper claim it's afraid of. Trellis illuminates the shadow.
**TL;DR:** Your essay's strongest argument is the one it's avoiding.
**Rationale:** The most interesting part of any piece is often what it refuses to say. A tool that surfaces the shadow makes every essay twice as honest.
**Why pick this one:** It offers a genuinely novel value proposition no writing tool currently names.
**Outline (6 beats):**
1. The concept of an essay's shadow.
2. How skilled writers sense the shadow but can't systematize finding it.
3. Why avoidance is structurally invisible to the writer.
4. Trellis's branching logic as a shadow-detector.
5. The moment a generated node reveals the claim you were dodging.
6. The essay that says the thing.

### 4. The Absence
**Title:** The Absence
**Summary:** The essay that doesn't exist has a shape—you can feel its outline in the negative space of your thinking. Trellis renders the absence visible.
**TL;DR:** The essay is defined by what's missing. Trellis fills the hole.
**Rationale:** Writers often know the shape of what they want to say by the shape of what they haven't said yet. That negative-space knowledge is real and usable.
**Why pick this one:** It's the most literary framing, which suits a literary audience.
**Outline (5 beats):**
1. The phantom essay: you know it's there because you can feel where it isn't.
2. Negative-space knowledge as a real cognitive state.
3. Why traditional tools only let you build on what's present.
4. Trellis as a rendering engine for absence—each node a pixel of the invisible.
5. The essay that was defined by its gaps, now whole.

### 5. 1975
**Title:** 1975
**Summary:** In 1975, Ted Nelson described a vision of branching, non-linear writing that never shipped. Trellis is what 1975 promised.
**TL;DR:** This isn't a new idea. It's a 50-year-old idea that finally works.
**Rationale:** Grounding Trellis in computing history lends it gravitas and frames it as the fulfillment of a known, respected vision rather than a novelty.
**Why pick this one:** It connects the product to a lineage, which is more persuasive than claiming innovation ex nihilo.
**Outline (7 beats):**
1. Ted Nelson, 1975, and the dream of branching literature.
2. Why the vision stalled—technical, cultural, and economic barriers.
3. The fifty-year gap and what filled it (linear tools, linear writing).
4. What Nelson got right and what he couldn't have predicted.
5. The technologies that finally make branching writing viable.
6. Trellis as the working version of the 1975 vision.
7. What it means to write in the future Nelson imagined.

### 6. The Rehearsal
**Title:** The Rehearsal
**Summary:** Writing an essay is a performance. Trellis is the rehearsal space—a low-stakes, repeatable, private arena where you find the argument before you have to deliver it.
**TL;DR:** You don't write the essay. You rehearse it until it's ready to write itself.
**Rationale:** The performance anxiety of writing is real and underaddressed. Framing the tool as a rehearsal removes the pressure of the blank page.
**Why pick this one:** It speaks to the emotional experience of writing, which is where the friction actually lives.
**Outline (6 beats):**
1. Writing as performance and the terror of the opening night draft.
2. Why you can't rehearse with a blank document.
3. The rehearsal space: private, repeatable, low-stakes.
4. How Trellis's branching structure lets you try the argument eight ways.
5. The transition from rehearsal to performance—when the essay is ready.
6. Opening night: the essay that was already performed.

### 7. When
**Title:** When
**Summary:** The question isn't whether to write the essay—it's when. Trellis answers 'when' by making 'now' the only remaining option.
**TL;DR:** The essay is ready when you are. Trellis makes you ready now.
**Rationale:** Procrastination is a timing problem disguised as a quality problem. A tool that collapses the distance between 'not yet' and 'now' solves the real friction.
**Why pick this one:** It's the most action-oriented framing, designed to convert intent into execution.
**Outline (6 beats):**
1. The perpetual 'not yet' and why it feels responsible.
2. How 'when' becomes 'never' without intervention.
3. The illusion of a better time to write.
4. Trellis as a timing-collapser: the five-minute 'now'.
5. The discovery that the essay was ready before you thought it was.
6. The essay written today instead of someday.

### 8. Tuition
**Title:** Tuition
**Summary:** Every essay you don't write is a lesson you don't learn. Trellis is tuition you pay to yourself—the act of writing as education, not just output.
**TL;DR:** The essay teaches you. Trellis is how you enroll.
**Rationale:** People undervalue writing because they see it as production rather than learning. Reframing it as tuition-inverts the value: the writer is the primary beneficiary.
**Why pick this one:** It shifts the motivation from external (audience, publication) to internal (self-knowledge), which is a more reliable trigger.
**Outline (8 beats):**
1. The misconception: writing is for readers.
2. The counter: writing is for the writer, always.
3. The lesson embedded in every unwritten essay.
4. Why you can't learn the lesson without writing the thing.
5. Traditional tools as tuition you can't afford—the cost of finding out.
6. Trellis as a scholarship: cheap, guided, and guaranteed to teach.
7. The moment the generated node teaches you something about yourself.
8. The essay as a degree in your own thinking. <!-- [^f2287-fc78b] -->
